The one distinction that matters

Every tool on this list can price a harness or cable assembly: material plus labor plus margin. The question is whether it checks the build before it prices it, or just prices the BOM it was given.

The checks are public knowledge, set by the standards and the connector makers’ own data:

  • Workmanship class. IPC/WHMA-A-620 is the acceptance standard for cable and wire harness assemblies. It has three product classes, general (1), dedicated service (2) and high performance or harsh environment (3), and the customer specifies which applies. A stricter class means tighter acceptance criteria and more inspection, which is labor the quote should carry.
  • Contact and wire. Each crimp contact or terminal is made for a range of wire gauges and insulation diameters, and needs the crimp tooling its maker specifies. A contact quoted onto a wire it can’t accept is an unbuildable line.
  • Seals. Sealed connectors take wire within the seal’s insulation-diameter range. The right gauge with the wrong insulation still won’t seal.
  • Connector and cable. Pole count, coding and contact rating have to match the cable and the job: an Ethernet run is only as fast as the connector coding allows, and a conductor has to be sized for the current it carries.

A tool that prices the BOM finds those problems at the bench or at inspection, after the PO, when the fix comes out of the margin you quoted. A tool that checks the build moves the discovery to the quote, when the fix is a different part number on the line.

A priced BOM is not a buildable assembly. The expensive mistake isn’t the missing label; it’s the contact that can’t take the wire.

The demo test

Bring one real RFQ, names removed, and ask each vendor to quote it live. Then check:

  1. Class. Leave the IPC/WHMA-A-620 class out. Does the tool ask, or quietly assume? Change it to Class 3: does the labor change?
  2. Contact and gauge. Specify a wire gauge outside the contact’s rated range. Is it flagged before pricing, or priced?
  3. Seal fit. On a sealed connector, give a wire whose insulation is outside the seal range. Does anything catch it?
  4. Data rate. Ask for 10 Gbit/s Ethernet on a connector coding that can’t carry it. Does the tool refuse and offer the alternative?
  5. Sources. Can it show the catalog page or the standard behind each check and each refusal?
  6. Completeness. Testing, labeling, documentation and certification lines all on the quote.

Any vendor, including us, should be able to run that on your RFQ. If your spreadsheet and your estimator pass all six, you may not need any of these tools.

Where Nteli is the wrong choice

  • Your RFQs are large drawing packages with hundreds of circuits, and the work is extracting the BOM and wire list from prints. Cableteque is built for that.
  • You need formboards, cut lists and production documents from the same system. HarnessSuite, Zuken Harness Builder or a harness CAD tool like RapidHarness covers that ground.
  • You already design in E3.series or carry KBL/HCV data and want cost straight from it. Zuken Harness Builder or Siemens Capital Harness Costing will fit your data.
  • You’re the OEM buyer checking what suppliers charge. aPriori is built for should-cost.
