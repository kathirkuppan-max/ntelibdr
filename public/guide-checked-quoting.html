Which quoting tools check for engineering errors before pricing?

The short answer: most quoting tools check that a quote is complete (every option picked, every combination allowed), not that it is correct (the breaker sized for the load, the cable rated for the current, the cooling matched to the heat). Three kinds of tool check some of the engineering: constraint-based configurators such as Tacton, instant-quote platforms such as Protolabs and Xometry for the parts they make, and rule engines such as Nteli that size and check engineered systems from the request, then price from your price book.

Complete is not correct

A quote can pass every rule in a CPQ and still describe something that can't be built. The configurator allowed the combination; nobody checked the numbers behind it. That gap costs real money: a panel quoted with a main breaker too small for its motors, a cable assembly whose connector can't carry the data rate, a data center pod whose cooling is sized for 25 kW racks when the racks shed 40 kW. Each one is caught later, by an engineer, after the price has gone out. Or it isn't caught.

The expensive errors aren't missing options. They're valid-looking quotes for products that don't work.

Three kinds of check

  • Option compatibility: can these options be chosen together? This is what CPQ product rules do, and most quoting tools stop here.
  • Manufacturability: can this part be made as drawn? Instant-quote platforms run design-for-manufacturing checks on uploaded CAD for machined, molded or printed parts.
  • Engineering sizing against codes and ratings: does this system work? Motor currents from NEC tables, short-circuit current rating against the available fault current, conductor ampacity, connector ratings, capacity left after redundancy, cooling against heat load.

What a checked quote looks like

Examples from the engine's public demos:

  • Control panels: motor currents taken from NEC Table 430.250, the panel's SCCR checked against the available fault current, and the main breaker kept within the NEC 409.21(C) limit. UL 508A panels →
  • Cable assemblies: a request for a D-coded M12 at gigabit speed is refused, with the reason: that connector has four contacts and stops at 100 Mbit/s. Cable assemblies →
  • Data center pods: UPS, generators, switchgear and cooling sized from one line and each checked for capacity after redundancy, so a 40 kW rack never ships with 25 kW of cooling. Data center pods →
  • NRE for medical devices: the device class, sterility, patient contact and electronics decide which deliverables the quote must carry. NRE services →

In each case the check runs before the price is calculated, and every size and every refusal shows its source.

Five tests to run in a demo

  • Ask for something that should fail. An over-limit load, an incompatible connector. Does the tool refuse and say why, or quote it anyway?
  • Ask where a number came from. A checked quote can point to the table, rating or catalog page behind each size and price.
  • Quote the same facts twice. The same request should give the same quote, every time.
  • Name a standard it doesn't cover. If your RFQ calls out NFPA 79 or CSA, does the quote say what wasn't checked, or stay silent?
  • Change one input. Raise the fault current or the rack density, and see what gets re-sized and re-priced.

Honest limits

A rule engine catches the errors its rules cover, and nothing else. It should tell you which standards it did not check, and your engineer still confirms the design before build: the engine checks constraints; it is not a certification body. And if your products are simple and catalog-priced, you don't need any of this. A price list or a basic CPQ covers you. If what you need is CAD-level manufacturability feedback on parts you are buying, an instant-quote platform is the right tool.
