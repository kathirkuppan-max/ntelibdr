Pod estimating: first-pass sizing without burning the week.

The short answer: a pod estimate is an engineering pass disguised as a price. kW per rack drives the PDUs; redundancy drives the UPS count; the UPS drives the switchgear and generators; density drives the cooling; and the cooling feeds back into the electrical load. Estimate them separately and the pieces disagree. The job is sizing them together — and pricing the result as one BOM.

Why the first pass eats the week

Prefab pod, e-house and power-skid builders live on bids where the RFQ says something like “1 MW, 40 racks, Tier-appropriate redundancy” and the customer wants a number this week. Getting to that number means a circular sizing exercise:

  • kW per rack → PDUs and feeds, and everything upstream of them.
  • Redundancy → UPS: N, N+1 or 2N changes the module count, battery strings and step-down transformers per side — not by a percentage, by topology.
  • Load → generators and switchgear, with interrupting ratings checked at the voltage, not assumed.
  • Density → cooling: CRAH units, coolant distribution, rear-door heat exchangers — sized against the heat each rack actually sheds. The classic miss: a 40 kW rack quoted with 25 kW of cooling, discovered after the steel is ordered.
  • Plus the parts that get forgotten at pricing time: containment, row busway, commissioning levels 1–3.

Done by hand, that's days of a senior engineer's time — spent on a bid you may not win. The estimating desk becomes the growth constraint not because demand is short, but because first passes are expensive.

The expensive part of a pod bid isn't the pricing. It's the engineering pass you do before you know if the bid is real.

What good pod estimating looks like

  • One system model, not six spreadsheets: racks, power train and cooling in a single model where a change in one resizes the others.
  • Redundancy as logic, not a multiplier: N+1 and 2N carried through module counts, transformers and switchgear topology.
  • Checks that refuse, with reasons: the estimate should flag the rack whose heat exceeds its cooling — before the quote ships, not after.
  • A BOM your engineers can defend: every size traceable to a rating or a rule, so the first pass survives contact with the customer's consultant.
  • Speed that matches bid economics: cheap first passes mean you can afford to answer more RFQs — and reserve the engineering week for bids that shortlist.
