Thinking · Revenue operations

A ledger, not a dashboard.

By Kathiravan Kuppan, Founder & CEO — built configurators and pricing systems at Cisco, Dell and IBM · November 2026

Finance solved its trust problem 600 years ago with double entry bookkeeping. Every transaction was recorded twice, so errors had nowhere to hide.

Revenue operations still runs on single entry.

The CRM records its version of the deal. The ERP records another. Billing keeps a third, and the channel program a fourth. Each system is internally consistent, yet nobody is responsible for whether they agree. We built an entire RevOps discipline around pipeline dashboards and forecast accuracy, and we left everything after the signature essentially unaudited.

Where the money actually goes

Not in dramatic failures, but in small divergences between systems that each look fine on their own. A quote says one number and the order is entered at another. A discount runs deeper than peer deals, approved by habit rather than by decision. A chargeback gets paid because verifying it costs more than the credit memo. An invoice is billed off the contract price, and customers never call when the error favors them.

None of this is a new idea. Finance teams know it as price waterfall analysis and revenue leakage, and analysts at MGI Research frame fixing it as worth 2–4% in added revenue and cash flow. The illustrative model below lands in the same range, at about 3% of revenue; its assumptions are all published and editable. But don't take anyone's benchmark on trust, ours included. The number that matters is yours, and it comes from your own data.

The ledger, itemized

This is why every Nteli engagement starts with a Leak Ledger: join every quote, order, invoice and claim on one deal ID, then itemize what is slipping, line by line. The model below shows what the ledger looks like for an illustrative mid-market manufacturer — around $90M revenue, dealer and distributor channels, an acquired division on a second ERP.

It separates two kinds of money, because a finance team will. Recoverable cash is money you can still collect: invoices billed off contract can be corrected, overpaid rebates clawed back, unsupported claims disputed. Preventable margin is money already gone: you can't take back last year's discounts or rework, only stop the next ones.

Two of these lines are commercial decisions as often as errors. A deep discount or a concession at the close may have been the right call. The ledger calls them unexplained until someone owns the reason; the point is visibility, not blame.

Almost none of this appears on any report, because every report is drawn from a single system's version of the truth. The divergences live between the systems — which is exactly where nobody looks.

“I can do this in a spreadsheet”

You can add it up in a spreadsheet. The total is the easy part. What a spreadsheet struggles with is everything before the total:

  • → The join. The quote, the order, the invoice and the distributor's claim don't share an ID. Distributor part numbers and end-customer names don't match your master data. Matching them is the work.
  • → Every line, not a sample. Thousands of claim and invoice lines a month, each checked against its contract and resale record, every month.
  • → Before the money moves. A spreadsheet finds last quarter's leak. A control stops it before the credit memo posts or the quote goes out.

So here's our offer: bring your spreadsheet. We'll run the same quarter, you on yours and us on ours. If your spreadsheet finds the same dollars, you don't need us.

Quote to cash is a reconciliation discipline

This is why I believe quote to cash is not a software category. The category fragmented years ago: CPQ vendors solved quoting, billing vendors solved invoicing, rebate vendors solved programs. Each silo got better, and the joins between them stayed unowned.

The unit of management is not the funnel stage or the invoice. It is the deal, threaded from quote to cash, with every line checked against the agreement behind it. A ledger, not a dashboard.

Recovery does not come from another dashboard. It comes from checks that run before the money moves: configurations validated against cited rules before the quote goes out, orders built from the accepted quote, invoices matched to the contract behind them, claims matched to agreements or disputed with evidence. The result is a recovery report a CFO can trace, line by line, down to the deal.

Double entry did not make merchants better at selling. It made their numbers trustworthy enough to build banks on. Revenue operations should be held to the same standard.

If you cannot trace a dollar from quote to cash, you do not have revenue operations. You have revenue observations.
