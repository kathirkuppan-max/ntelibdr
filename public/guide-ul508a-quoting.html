UL 508A control panel quoting software: what to look for.

The short answer: quoting software for UL 508A panel shops is only as good as the rules it checks — motor currents from NEC Table 430.250 with the main per 430.24/409.20, SCCR against available fault current, starters and drives from published ratings, and enclosure/cooling rules. If the tool can't cite why it sized something, it's a calculator, not an estimator.

Why panel quotes go wrong

A control panel RFQ usually arrives as a messy email: a motor schedule, a voltage that may or may not be stated, an enclosure environment implied rather than specified. The estimator re-keys the same starter, breaker and enclosure picks for every quote — and the checks that make the panel buildable live in their head.

The failure modes are always the same, and each one comes out of margin on the floor or in the field:

  • The main is undersized because motor currents were summed from nameplates instead of NEC Table 430.250, or 430.24's 125% rule was skipped.
  • SCCR doesn't meet the available fault current — the single most common reason an AHJ or customer bounces a panel.
  • The interrupting rating is quoted at the wrong voltage — a breaker's kA rating at 240 V isn't its rating at 480 V.
  • Cooling violates the enclosure rating — a fan cut into a NEMA 3R, 4 or 4X box voids the rating that was the whole point.
  • The quote and the submittal disagree, because they were built twice by different people.

The checklist: what the software must verify

  • Supply voltage — 208, 240, 480 or 600 V — asked explicitly when the RFQ doesn't say, never assumed.
  • Motor current from NEC Table 430.250, main sized to NEC 430.24 / 409.20.
  • SCCR vs. available fault current, and the main breaker's interrupting rating at the supply voltage.
  • Starters (full-voltage, reversing, soft) and drives sized from published manufacturer ratings, not rules of thumb.
  • Enclosure type (NEMA 1, 12, 3R, 4, 4X) and cooling rules — no fans on 3R/4/4X.
  • A source for every answer — the code section or the catalog page, so an engineer can trust a number or a refusal without re-deriving it.

One honest boundary: software checks constraints; it is not a certification body. The UL 508A label still comes from your shop's program and procedures.

Spreadsheets vs. CPQ vs. AI-era quoting

Spreadsheets are where most shops are: free, familiar, and dependent on the one senior estimator nobody can afford to lose. A hand-built panel quote takes most of half a day, which caps a shop at roughly two quotes per estimator per day — the real constraint on how many RFQs you can answer.

Traditional CPQ (the kind Cisco and Dell run) fixes the checking problem but assumes a dedicated team to author and maintain rules, and a seller clicking through a configurator. That's an enterprise program, not a 20–300-person shop's tool.

AI-era quoting inverts the interface: paste the RFQ, the spec or the customer email into an AI assistant, and a deterministic rules engine does the sizing and checking behind it. The AI runs the conversation and asks for what's missing; the engine computes from cited rules and never invents a number. The same quote produces the submittal sheet your customer approves before the PO — ratings, a dimensioned layout, a one-line diagram and the bill of materials. Switchgear and MCC-sized jobs route to your engineers with the details already gathered.
