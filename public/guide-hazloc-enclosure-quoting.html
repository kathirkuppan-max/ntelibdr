Hazardous location enclosure quoting: what every quote must check.

By Kathiravan Kuppan · Published November 16, 2026
The short answer: a hazardous-location enclosure quote is only right if the configuration behind it is right. Before any price, the quote has to settle the area classification (Class I Division 1 or 2, or Zone 1 or 2), the gas group, the temperature class, the protection method, the purge type if the enclosure is purged, and the certification of every component and of the assembly. Get one of those wrong and the price is for a product that can’t be installed.

Why hazloc enclosure quotes go wrong

Requests for explosion-proof enclosures, purged panels and hazardous-location junction boxes rarely arrive complete. The RFQ says “for a refinery” or “Class 1 area” and leaves out the Division, the group or the temperature class. The estimator fills in the gaps from memory, and the checks that make the product installable live in one or two senior people’s heads.

The ways a hazloc quote goes wrong repeat from job to job:

  • The classification is assumed, not stated. A Division 2 price goes out for what turns out to be a Division 1 location, or the other way round, and the job is either unsafe or overpriced.
  • The gas group is missed. An enclosure listed for Group D (propane-type atmospheres) isn’t suitable for Group B (hydrogen). Equipment has to be listed for the specific group present.
  • The temperature class doesn’t clear the gas. The equipment’s T-code has to stay below the autoignition temperature of the gas or vapor present, and a drive, power supply or heater inside a sealed box raises surface temperature.
  • The heat load doesn’t fit the protection method. A sealed explosionproof box can’t simply take a fan, so a hot load can force a larger enclosure or a different method, such as purging.
  • The listing gets voided after the quote. Extra entries or cut-outs added to a listed explosionproof enclosure outside the manufacturer’s program can void the listing that was the point of buying it.
  • The wrong certificate goes to the wrong market. An ATEX or IECEx certificate is offered for a US site, or a Division-marked product for a Zone-classified one.
  • Seals, breathers and drains are left off the bill of materials and show up later as a change order or a margin hit.

What a hazardous-location quote must check

None of this is proprietary. It comes from public codes and standards, and it is what an experienced hazloc estimator checks before pricing. A quoting tool should check the same things, ask when the RFQ doesn’t say, and never assume.

1. Area classification

  • Class and Division under NEC Article 500: Class I (flammable gases and vapors), Class II (combustible dusts), Class III (ignitable fibers and flyings). Division 1 means ignitable concentrations can exist in normal operation; Division 2 means they are expected only under abnormal conditions.
  • Zones under NEC Article 505 for gases (Zone 0, 1, 2) and Article 506 for dusts (Zone 20, 21, 22).
  • Who classifies the area: the owner and their engineers, typically using NFPA 497 (gases and vapors), NFPA 499 (dusts), or API RP 500 and RP 505 at petroleum facilities. The manufacturer quotes to the classification; it shouldn’t invent one.

2. Gas or dust group

  • Division system: Group A (acetylene), Group B (hydrogen), Group C (ethylene), Group D (propane); dust Groups E (metal), F (carbonaceous) and G (other dusts such as grain and plastics).
  • Zone system: Group IIC (acetylene and hydrogen), IIB (ethylene), IIA (propane); dust Groups IIIA (combustible flyings), IIIB (non-conductive dust) and IIIC (conductive dust).

3. Temperature class

  • T-codes set the maximum surface temperature: T1 450 °C, T2 300 °C, T3 200 °C, T4 135 °C, T5 100 °C, T6 85 °C. The marked T-code must be below the autoignition temperature of the specific material present.
  • Ambient range: ratings assume an ambient range (IEC 60079-0 uses −20 °C to +40 °C by default). Outdoor sites outside that range need equipment marked for it.

4. Protection method

  • Division methods: explosionproof (UL 1203), dust-ignitionproof, purged and pressurized (NFPA 496), intrinsically safe (UL 913), and nonincendive for Division 2 (UL 121201), plus hermetically sealed contacts and others the NEC recognizes.
  • Zone methods from the IEC 60079 series and their ANSI/UL 60079 counterparts: flameproof “d” (60079-1), pressurized “p” (60079-2), increased safety “e” (60079-7), intrinsic safety “i” (60079-11), encapsulation “m” (60079-18).
  • What’s inside decides the method. Arcing contacts, drives and heat-producing equipment narrow the options. An explosionproof enclosure is built to contain an internal explosion and keep it from igniting the atmosphere outside, which is why it can’t simply be vented for cooling.

5. Purge type, when the enclosure is purged

  • Type X (NFPA 496): Division 1 or Zone 1 down to unclassified inside the enclosure, with automatic de-energization when protective pressure is lost.
  • Type Y: Division 1 down to Division 2. The equipment inside must itself be suitable for Division 2.
  • Type Z: Division 2 down to unclassified.
  • Behind every purge line on the quote: a clean protective-gas supply, a purge before power-up, and the pressure monitoring the type requires. Check the edition of NFPA 496 the jurisdiction enforces.

6. Certification of components and of the assembly

  • United States: equipment listed by a Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory for the Class, Division or Zone, group and T-code. Zone equipment is typically listed to the ANSI/UL 60079 series and marked AEx. UL 698A covers control panels in unclassified locations whose intrinsically safe circuits extend into the hazardous area.
  • Canada: the Canadian Electrical Code, Section 18, with certified equipment.
  • European Union: the ATEX equipment directive, 2014/34/EU, where equipment Categories 1, 2 and 3 line up with Zones 0, 1 and 2 for gas.
  • International: IECEx certification to the IEC 60079 series.
  • Wiring and sealing: conduit seals, drains and breathers per NEC 501.15 for Class I, or listed cable glands for Zone installations.

One honest boundary. Software can check a configuration against the stated classification and the published ratings of listed components. It doesn’t certify equipment or replace the listing body, and the authority having jurisdiction still decides what gets installed.

Hazloc enclosure estimation vs. quotation

The two words get used interchangeably, but they are separate steps. Estimation works out what the enclosure or panel will cost you to build: the listed enclosure, the components inside, the purge system, seals, glands, breathers, labor and certification costs, each from your own price book and routings. The quotation is what the customer sees: the price, the scope, the exclusions, the classification it was built to, and the documents that go with it.

In hazardous locations the step between them matters more than anywhere else. An estimate built on the wrong group or T-code is a confident price for a product that can’t ship. The useful kind of tool does both steps from one request, with the classification checks in between.

Spreadsheets vs. CPQ vs. a checked-quote engine

Spreadsheets are where most specialty manufacturers start. They cost nothing and everyone knows them, but they check nothing. The classification logic sits with whoever built the sheet, and every quote depends on that person being available.

Manufacturer product selectors configure one brand’s catalog for buyers ordering that brand. They help a buyer pick a box. They don’t quote your build, your labor or your margins.

Traditional CPQ can enforce rules, but it expects a dedicated team to write and maintain them and a seller clicking through screens. Hazloc rules have to be written and kept current by your own team, which makes it an enterprise program rather than a tool for a specialty shop.

A checked-quote engine reverses the order. The configuration is checked against the classification first, and only a valid configuration is priced from your rules. When a fact is missing, such as the Division, the group or what goes inside the box, it asks instead of guessing. When a request doesn’t fit the classification it refuses, says why, and offers the closest valid option. Frequently asked questions

What should hazardous location enclosure quoting software check?

Before it prices anything: the area classification (Class and Division under NEC Article 500, or Zone under Article 505 or 506), the gas or dust group, the temperature class against the autoignition temperature of the material present, the protection method, the purge type where purging is used, what is inside the enclosure, and whether each component and the assembly carry the right certification for the market where it will be installed.

What is the difference between Type X, Type Y and Type Z purge?

NFPA 496 defines three types. Type X takes a Division 1 (or Zone 1) enclosure interior to unclassified and must automatically de-energize the equipment when protective pressure is lost. Type Y takes Division 1 to Division 2, so the equipment inside must be suitable for Division 2. Type Z takes Division 2 to unclassified. Types Y and Z don’t require automatic power removal; loss of pressure must be detected and signaled.

Can an ATEX or IECEx certified enclosure be quoted for a US installation?

Not on that certificate alone. In the United States, hazardous-location equipment is listed by a Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory. Zone-rated equipment is typically listed to the ANSI/UL 60079 series and marked AEx. Many products are dual-certified, and the authority having jurisdiction has the final say.

Does a Class I Division 2 enclosure have to be explosionproof?

Not always. Equipment without make-and-break contacts or hot surfaces, and nonincendive equipment, can often go in a general-purpose enclosure. Arcing contacts generally need another method, such as an explosionproof enclosure, hermetically sealed contacts, nonincendive ratings or Type Z purging. NEC Article 501 sets the rules.

Does quoting software certify a hazardous-location enclosure?

No. It can check a configuration against the stated classification and the ratings of listed components, and refuse or flag it when they don’t match. Listing comes from the testing laboratory, and acceptance on site comes from the authority having jurisdiction.
