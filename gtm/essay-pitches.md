## 1. RevOps Co-op — Creator Guild

**Subject:** Byline pitch: revenue ops still runs on single-entry bookkeeping

Hi RevOps Co-op team,

I'd like to contribute through the Creator Guild. The piece I have in mind argues that RevOps
built a whole discipline around the pipeline and left everything after the signature
unaudited: the CRM, ERP, billing and channel systems each record their own version of a deal,
and nobody owns whether they agree. Finance fixed that problem 600 years ago with double-entry
bookkeeping; revenue operations hasn't.

It includes a worked ten-line "leak ledger" for a mid-market manufacturer, clearly labelled as
an illustrative model, and closes on a line your readers will either quote or argue with: if
you can't trace a dollar from quote to cash, you don't have revenue operations, you have
revenue observations.

The current version is here: https://nteli.io/thinking/a-ledger-not-a-dashboard. Happy to adapt
it for your audience, or write a companion piece on running the reconciliation in practice.

## 2. IndustryWeek — Pitch A (new, first-run piece)

**Subject:** Contributed article pitch: a complete quote is not a correct one

Hi [editor name from the contributors page],

I'd like to pitch an original contributed article (about 900 words) for manufacturing leaders.

Working title: **"A complete quote is not a correct one."**

Most custom manufacturers now protect quotes for completeness: the crate, the FAT, the wiring
hours, the price changes since the quote went out. Fewer check correctness: whether the
configuration on the quote can be built, certified and installed as priced. A control panel
quoted at a 35 kA rating that ships into 65 kA of available fault current is complete and
wrong, and the cost lands at commissioning, not at quoting.

The article would cover:
- the difference between completeness errors and correctness errors, with examples from
  panels, pumps and electrical distribution equipment;
- why correctness errors cost more (they surface after the PO, on the floor or at inspection);
- what checking for correctness requires at the quote desk: cited code rules, versioned, with
  someone accountable for each rule change;
- a short self-assessment readers can run on their last ten quotes.

It hasn't been published anywhere. The ideas and examples come from ten years of building
product configurators and pricing systems at Cisco, Dell and IBM. I now run Nteli, which
builds quoting software for configure-to-order manufacturers.

not in the pitch.)

## 3. Manufacturing Dive — Pitch B (new piece; confirm originality rule on their page)

**Subject:** Opinion pitch: your quote desk is absorbing rule changes from four directions

Hi Manufacturing Dive editors,

I'd like to submit an opinion piece on a pressure most manufacturing leaders feel but rarely
name: the rules behind every quote are changing from four directions at once.

- Efficiency standards: DOE's next distribution-transformer efficiency levels take effect in
  2029, with a further review already underway.
- Trade: Section 232 tariffs reprice steel and aluminum content mid-quote.
- Codes: each NEC cycle changes what can be installed where.
- Supply: 30- to 60-week lead times turn a quote's assumptions into a bet.

My argument: the companies that handle this well treat their quoting rules like a governed
asset, with versions, a named owner for each change, and replay on past quotes to see what
the change would have cost, instead of spreadsheets each estimator maintains alone.

I've built configurators and pricing systems at Cisco, Dell and IBM and now run Nteli in
Austin. The piece would be original to Manufacturing Dive.

Kathiravan Kuppan

## 4. MDM — Pitch C (podcast)

**Subject:** Podcast pitch: why distributor claims go unverified, and how to fix it without damaging the relationship

Hi MDM team,

A podcast topic your distributor audience lives with every quarter: ship-and-debit claims and
chargebacks between manufacturers and distributors.

Most manufacturers pay distributor claims largely unverified, because checking each line
against the agreement and the resale data costs more than the credit. When they do start
checking, it can feel adversarial to the distributor. I'd like to talk through:
- why claims go unverified (the agreement, the transaction and the resale data live in three
  different systems);
- what line-level validation actually checks (EDI 867 resale records, design registrations
  and special pricing agreements, inventory);
- how to dispute with evidence so the relationship survives, and what distributors gain from
  cleaner, faster settlement.

I run Nteli, which does this reconciliation for manufacturers, and spent years before that
building pricing and configuration systems at Cisco, Dell and IBM. Happy to keep it
educational rather than a product pitch.

Kathiravan Kuppan
Founder & CEO, Nteli · https://nteli.io
